When a massive asset manager requires the conversion of billions of dollars to allocate capital globally, the execution requires strict mechanical precision. A single market order of that magnitude would instantly consume all available liquidity, causing severe price distortion and unacceptable execution costs. To prevent this market impact, the asset manager transfers the execution risk to a primary dealer, typically a Tier-1 global bank.

The primary dealer acts as a structural buffer between the massive capital and the public market. The first step in processing a massive transaction is internalization. The dealer attempts to match the buy order against existing sell orders within their own private liquidity network, bypassing the public exchange entirely. If the internal volume is insufficient to absorb the entire request, the dealer must access the broader foreign exchange market without exposing the total size of the operation.

This is achieved through mechanical fragmentation. The primary dealer deploys execution algorithms to slice the massive remaining volume into hundreds of smaller blocks. These micro-orders are systematically fed into public electronic communication networks and dark pools over an extended period. By distributing the volume, the dealer prevents sudden liquidity shocks and hides the total footprint from other market participants. When retail traders observe recurring absorption or persistent volume at specific price levels on a liquidity heatmap, they are often watching the mechanical execution of these fragmented institutional blocks.

The market is simply processing a hidden, large-scale capital transfer.
