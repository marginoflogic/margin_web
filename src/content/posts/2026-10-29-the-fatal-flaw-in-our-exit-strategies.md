We all make the same systemic error when we begin trading: we set profit targets based on what we want, rather than on what the market structure provides.
We are taught to focus strictly on a 1:2 or 1:3 risk-to-reward ratio for every single trade—or simply to follow the trend without setting a specific target. However, market architecture does not adapt to our personal financial models. By imposing an arbitrary mathematical target onto a chart, we are essentially trading based on a superficial illusion, completely ignoring the raw reality of how price actually moves.

Setting a target is not a financial wish; it is a rigorous protocol for exiting a position. The only logical place to exit a position is where opposing liquidity resides. If we are in a long position, our protocol must trigger exactly where early short sellers have placed their buy-stop orders. We must target the precise areas where the crowd is forced to capitulate. If the distance between our entry point and the next liquidity pool is too small, we cannot simply "magically" extend our target just to make the math work. Instead, we must abort the process.
The reason we struggle so much with exiting trades is that we trade based on our profit-and-loss outcomes rather than on raw data. We hold positions for too long because we want a larger win, or we exit too early because we fear losing what we have already gained. The structural objective completely eliminates this processing error. It functions as an automated system cool-down, triggering an exit before our emotions can override the protocol.

Separating our financial desires from our analytical exit decisions acts as a brutal system reset—yet it is the only way to survive in this environment.
