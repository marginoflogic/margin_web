There is a mechanical paradox at the core of institutional trading: the primary dealer is a neutral balancer of risk, yet operates as an aggressive hunter of liquidity.

Retail traders often struggle to reconcile these two facts. They assume that if a market maker doesn't care about market direction, they shouldn't care about retail stop losses. This reveals a fundamental misunderstanding of how an order book is cleared.

When a major institution executes a massive block order to buy, the dealer provides that liquidity by selling. In that fraction of a second, the dealer absorbs a massive, unhedged short position. They do not want this exposure. To neutralize their risk and balance the book, they must immediately buy an equal amount of the asset. They cannot wait passively; they must forcefully extract opposing volume from the market.

Where does an algorithm find a massive cluster of guaranteed sell orders? Right below an old low, where retail participants have predictably placed their stop losses.

The market maker drives the price into these zones not out of spite or personal vendetta against retail traders. They do it because retail liquidity is the operational fuel required to process institutional transactions. You are not being punished; you are simply being utilized as a counterparty of convenience.

Stop viewing the market as a battleground of geometric patterns. It is a logistical map of resting liquidity.

If you cannot identify where the fuel is stored, you are the fuel.
